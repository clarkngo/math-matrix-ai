Gaussian Distributions & Change of Variables

The bell curve shows up everywhere: heights, test scores, measurement errors. Learn to read it, compare against it, and reshape it.

📊 Intermediate⏱ ~2 hours🧮 Needs: Modules 5 and 7

Lesson overview

The Gaussian (or normal) distribution is the bell-shaped curve. Two numbers describe it completely: the mean $\mu$ (where the center is) and the standard deviation $\sigma$ (how wide it is). It's the default assumption for noise in almost every ML model, and Module 10 shows that assuming it leads directly to least-squares regression.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Use the 68–95–99.7 rule to estimate probabilities.
  • Compute z-scores to compare values on different scales.
  • Say what happens to $\mu$ and $\sigma$ when you shift and scale a variable.
  • Read a 2×2 covariance matrix as the shape of a point cloud.
  • Explain why a density changes height when you stretch it (change of variables).
You needMean and variance (Module 7); eigenvectors (Module 5) for the 2D part.
This unlocksProbabilistic regression (Module 10), Gaussian processes, and generative models.

The bell curve and the 68–95–99.7 rule

≈ 20 min

We write $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$: "$X$ follows a normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$". The curve's height at each point is the density:

$$p(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}.$$

You rarely need the formula itself. Read it as: the further $x$ is from $\mu$ (measured in units of $\sigma$), the faster the height drops.

Worked example · adult heights with $\mu = 170$ cm, $\sigma = 10$ cm
  1. About 68% fall within $\mu \pm \sigma$: between 160 and 180 cm
    One standard deviation either side.
  2. About 95% within $\mu \pm 2\sigma$: 150 to 190 cm
    Two standard deviations.
  3. About 99.7% within $\mu \pm 3\sigma$: 140 to 200 cm
    Almost everyone.
  4. Taller than 190 cm: $(100\% - 95\%) / 2 = 2.5\%$
    The 5% outside is split evenly between the two tails.
In real life

Factories use this for quality control. If bolts are meant to be 10 mm long with $\sigma = 0.1$ mm, almost every bolt (99.7%) will be between 9.7 and 10.3 mm. A bolt at 10.5 mm is 5 standard deviations off, so something has gone wrong with the machine. This is where the "Six Sigma" quality program got its name.

68–95–99.7: one, two, three steps of $\sigma$ from the middle.

z-scores: comparing apples and oranges

≈ 15 min

A z-score says how many standard deviations a value is from the mean: $z = \dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$. It converts any normal variable to the standard one, $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

Worked example · how unusual is 185 cm?
  1. $z = \dfrac{185 - 170}{10} = 1.5$
    15 cm above average, and each $\sigma$ is 10 cm.
  2. Between $z = 1$ (84th percentile) and $z = 2$ (97.5th)
    From the 68–95 rule: 50% + 34% below $z = 1$; 50% + 47.5% below $z = 2$.
A z-table gives about the 93rd percentile: taller than roughly 93% of people.
In real life

Which is better: 1300 on a test with mean 1050 and $\sigma = 200$, or 27 on a test with mean 21 and $\sigma = 5$? The z-scores are $\frac{1300 - 1050}{200} = 1.25$ and $\frac{27 - 21}{5} = 1.2$, so the first is slightly stronger. In ML, "standardizing" every feature to z-scores is a standard first step, so features measured in dollars and in years can be compared fairly.

A z-score answers: "how many $\sigma$'s from normal?"

Shifting and scaling a Gaussian

≈ 15 min

If $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $Y = aX + b$, then $Y$ is also normal, with

$$\text{mean } a\mu + b, \qquad \text{standard deviation } |a|\sigma, \qquad \text{variance } a^2\sigma^2.$$
Worked example · $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $Y = 2X + 5$
  1. Mean: $2(0) + 5 = 5$
    Shifting by $b$ moves the center; scaling by $a$ scales it.
  2. Standard deviation: $2 \times 1 = 2$
    Adding 5 doesn't change the width; multiplying by 2 doubles it.
  3. Variance: $2^2 \times 1 = 4$
    Variance is in squared units, so it scales by $a^2$.
$Y \sim \mathcal{N}(5, 4)$
In real life

Daily temperatures with mean 20 °C and $\sigma = 5$ °C, converted with $F = 1.8C + 32$, have mean $1.8(20) + 32 = 68$ °F and $\sigma = 1.8 \times 5 = 9$ °F. The +32 only shifts the center; the 1.8 stretches both the center and the spread.

Adding shifts the bell; multiplying stretches it.

Two variables: covariance and the tilted ellipse

≈ 25 min

With two variables, one number isn't enough to describe the spread. The covariance matrix $\Sigma$ holds each variable's variance on the diagonal and their covariance (how they move together) off the diagonal. A cloud of points from a 2D Gaussian forms an ellipse, and $\Sigma$'s eigenvectors (Module 5) are the ellipse's axes.

Worked example · $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}4&3\\3&4\end{bmatrix}$
  1. Each variable has variance 4, so $\sigma = 2$
    Diagonal entries.
  2. Covariance $= 3 > 0$
    Positive: when one is high, the other tends to be high. The cloud tilts up and to the right.
  3. Eigenvalues $4 \pm 3 = 7$ and $1$, with eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$
    Same pattern as Module 5's $\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$.
  4. Long axis along $(1, 1)$ with spread $\sqrt{7} \approx 2.65$; short axis along $(1, -1)$ with spread $1$
    A cigar-shaped cloud along the diagonal.
In real life

Height and weight have positive covariance: taller people tend to weigh more, so a scatter plot of the two is a tilted ellipse. Investors use covariance between stocks to build portfolios: combining stocks with low or negative covariance smooths out the ups and downs.

Diagonal = how much each one wobbles. Off-diagonal = whether they wobble together.
Try it yourself (click to reveal the answer)
What shape is the cloud for $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}9&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$?
Covariance 0, so no tilt. Spread $\sqrt{9} = 3$ horizontally and $1$ vertically: a flat ellipse lying along the x-axis, 3 times wider than it is tall.

Change of variables: stretching a density

≈ 20 min

When you transform a random variable, the total probability must still add up to 1. So if you stretch the values out, the density must get lower to compensate. The correction factor is the Jacobian (in 1D, just the stretch factor).

Worked example · $X$ uniform on $[0, 1]$, and $Y = 2X$
  1. $p_X(x) = 1$ on $[0, 1]$
    A flat density. Area $= 1 \times 1 = 1$ ✓.
  2. $Y$ ranges over $[0, 2]$
    Doubling every value doubles the width.
  3. $p_Y(y) = p_X(y/2) \times \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}$
    Undo the map ($x = y/2$), then multiply by the stretch correction $\frac{dx}{dy} = \frac{1}{2}$.
  4. Area $= 2 \times \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ ✓
    Twice as wide, half as tall, same total.

In several dimensions the stretch factor becomes $|\det J|$, the determinant of the Jacobian matrix, which is the same "area multiplier" idea as Module 5.

In real life

Spread the same pat of butter over a slice of toast twice as long and the layer is half as thick. Population density works the same way: the same number of people spread over twice the area means half the people per square mile. AI image generators rely on this rule when they turn simple random noise into realistic images: every stretch or squeeze of the space must be tracked so probabilities still add to 1.

Same butter, bigger toast, thinner layer. The Jacobian keeps the total at 1.

Key terms

Gaussian / normal
The bell-shaped distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$.
z-score
$(x - \mu)/\sigma$: distance from the mean in standard deviations.
Covariance
How two variables move together; positive means up together.
Covariance matrix
Variances on the diagonal, covariances off it; describes the ellipse shape.
Jacobian
The stretch factor that corrects a density after a change of variables.
