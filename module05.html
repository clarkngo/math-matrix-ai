Determinants, Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors

Most directions get turned when a matrix acts on them. A few special ones only get stretched, and they reveal what the matrix really does.

📊 Intermediate⏱ ~2.5 hours🧮 Needs: Modules 1, 2, 4
Overview Determinant Eigenvectors Finding Them Power Iteration SVD & PCA Review

Lesson overview

Think of a matrix as a machine that moves every point in the plane (Module 1). Two questions describe the machine: how much does it stretch area? (the determinant) and which directions does it leave pointing the same way? (eigenvectors). Those directions are the key to PCA, Google's original PageRank, and compressing images.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Interpret a determinant as an area scale factor, and explain what 0 and negative values mean.
  • Check whether a vector is an eigenvector and find its eigenvalue.
  • Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a 2×2 matrix by hand.
  • Explain power iteration and why it finds the biggest eigenvector.
  • Describe what SVD and PCA do, and why they compress data.
You needMatrix × vector and the 2×2 determinant (Module 1); solving a quadratic $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.

The determinant: how much does area stretch?

≈ 20 min

Apply a matrix to the unit square (the square with corners $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(1,1)$). It becomes a parallelogram. The determinant is that parallelogram's area.

Worked example · $A = \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$
  1. $A(1, 0) = (2, 1), \quad A(0, 1) = (1, 2)$
    The square's two edges land on the columns of $A$.
  2. $\det A = 2\cdot2 - 1\cdot1 = 3$
    The $ad - bc$ formula from Module 1.
Every shape's area gets multiplied by 3 under this matrix.
DeterminantWhat it means
$> 1$Areas grow
between 0 and 1Areas shrink
$0$The plane gets squashed flat onto a line, information is lost, and there's no inverse
negativeThe shape is also flipped, like a mirror image
In real life

Resize a photo to 2× the width and 3× the height: the matrix is $\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{bmatrix}$, its determinant is 6, and the file has 6 times as many pixels. Mirror the photo and the determinant becomes negative. Squash it to a 1-pixel-tall strip and the determinant is 0: you can't un-squash it, because the detail is gone.

The determinant is the area multiplier. Zero means flattened beyond repair.
🎯

Eigenvectors: directions that only stretch

≈ 20 min

Most vectors change direction when you multiply by a matrix. An eigenvector is a vector whose direction stays the same; it only gets stretched by a number $\lambda$ (lambda), its eigenvalue:

$$A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}.$$
Worked example · test three vectors with $A = \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$
  1. $A(1, 1) = (2 + 1,\; 1 + 2) = (3, 3) = 3\,(1, 1)$
    Same direction, 3 times longer. Eigenvector, with $\lambda = 3$.
  2. $A(1, -1) = (2 - 1,\; 1 - 2) = (1, -1) = 1\,(1, -1)$
    Unchanged. Eigenvector, with $\lambda = 1$.
  3. $A(1, 0) = (2, 1)$
    $(2, 1)$ isn't a multiple of $(1, 0)$: the direction changed. Not an eigenvector.
This matrix stretches the diagonal $(1, 1)$ by 3 and leaves the anti-diagonal $(1, -1)$ alone. Notice $3 \times 1 = 3 = \det A$: the determinant is always the product of the eigenvalues.
In real life

Spin a globe: every point moves except those on the axis through the poles. That axis is an eigenvector of the rotation, with eigenvalue 1. Stretch a sheet of pizza dough by pulling left and right: horizontal lines stay horizontal (eigenvector, big $\lambda$), vertical lines stay vertical (eigenvector, $\lambda$ a bit under 1 as the dough thins), and diagonals get tilted.

Eigenvectors are the directions that don't turn, only stretch.
🔍

Finding eigenvalues by hand

≈ 25 min

Rewrite $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$ as $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$, where $I$ is the identity matrix (1s on the diagonal, 0s elsewhere). A nonzero $\mathbf{v}$ can only be sent to zero if the matrix $A - \lambda I$ squashes the plane, so its determinant must be 0.

Worked example · eigenvalues of $A = \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$
  1. $A - \lambda I = \begin{bmatrix}2 - \lambda & 1\\1 & 2 - \lambda\end{bmatrix}$
    Subtract $\lambda$ from the diagonal entries only.
  2. $\det = (2 - \lambda)^2 - 1 = 0$
    $ad - bc$ again.
  3. $(2 - \lambda)^2 = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; 2 - \lambda = \pm 1$
    Take the square root of both sides; keep both signs.
  4. $\lambda = 1 \;\text{ or }\; \lambda = 3$
    The two eigenvalues.
  5. For $\lambda = 3$: $\begin{bmatrix}-1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \Rightarrow v_1 = v_2 \Rightarrow \mathbf{v} = (1, 1)$
    Plug each eigenvalue back in and solve, like finding a kernel in Module 2.
You already know this

Step 2 is just a quadratic equation in $\lambda$. You could expand it to $\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = 0$ and factor it as $(\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 3) = 0$.

Try it yourself (click to reveal the answer)
Find the eigenvalues of $\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$.
$(4 - \lambda)(2 - \lambda) - 0 = 0$, so $\lambda = 4$ or $2$. For a diagonal matrix, the eigenvalues are just the diagonal entries, and the eigenvectors are the axes $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$.

Power iteration: multiply until it settles

≈ 15 min

Keep multiplying any starting vector by $A$. The part along the eigenvector with the biggest eigenvalue grows fastest, so the vector swings toward that direction.

Worked example · start at $(1, 0)$ with $A = \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$
  1. $A(1, 0) = (2, 1)$
    Ratio of entries: $2/1 = 2$.
  2. $A(2, 1) = (5, 4)$
    Ratio $1.25$.
  3. $A(5, 4) = (14, 13)$
    Ratio $1.08$, heading toward 1, which means the direction $(1, 1)$.
  4. $\lVert(14, 13)\rVert / \lVert(5, 4)\rVert = 19.1 / 6.4 \approx 2.98$
    Each step multiplies the length by about 3, the top eigenvalue.
Repeated multiplication found the top eigenvector $(1, 1)$ and eigenvalue $3$ without solving any equation.
In real life

Google's original PageRank was power iteration. Imagine millions of people clicking links at random; after many rounds, the share of people on each page settles down. That settled vector, the top eigenvector of the web's link matrix, ranks every page by importance. Viral content spreads the same way: repeated sharing amplifies whatever direction grows fastest.

Keep applying the matrix, and the strongest direction wins.

SVD and PCA: finding the directions that matter

≈ 30 min

Any matrix, even a non-square one, can be split into rotate → stretch → rotate. This is the singular value decomposition (SVD), $A = U\Sigma V^\top$. The stretch amounts are the singular values; they're the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^\top A$.

Worked example · singular values of $A = \begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix}$
  1. $A^\top A = \begin{bmatrix}3&4\\0&5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}25&20\\20&25\end{bmatrix}$
    Row-times-column, as in Module 1.
  2. Eigenvalues: $25 \pm 20 = 45$ and $5$
    Same pattern as the $\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ example: diagonal ± off-diagonal.
  3. $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{45} \approx 6.71, \quad \sigma_2 = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$
    The matrix stretches by 6.71 in its strongest direction and 2.24 in the other.

PCA (principal component analysis) applies the same idea to data: find the directions where the data varies most.

Worked example · PCA on five points

Points: $(-2, -2)$, $(-1, -1.2)$, $(0, 0.2)$, $(1, 0.8)$, $(2, 2.2)$. They lie almost on a diagonal line.

  1. Covariance matrix $\approx \begin{bmatrix}2.50&2.60\\2.60&2.74\end{bmatrix}$
    How much each coordinate spreads (diagonal) and how they move together (off-diagonal). Module 8 explains covariance in detail.
  2. Eigenvalues $\approx 5.22$ and $0.017$; top eigenvector $\approx (0.69, 0.72)$
    The top direction is the diagonal, as the picture suggests.
  3. $5.22 / (5.22 + 0.017) \approx 99.7\%$
    Share of all variation captured by that one direction.
Two numbers per point can be replaced by one (position along the diagonal) while losing only 0.3% of the information.
In real life
  • Image compression: a photo is a matrix of pixel brightness. Keep only the top 20 singular values and you can rebuild a recognizable image from a small fraction of the numbers.
  • Surveys: 20 customer-satisfaction questions often boil down to 2 or 3 PCA directions, like "overall happiness" and "price sensitivity".
  • Face recognition systems once used "eigenfaces", the top eigenvectors of thousands of face photos.
SVD = rotate, stretch, rotate. PCA keeps the stretchiest directions and drops the rest.

Key terms

Determinant
The factor by which a matrix scales area (or volume); negative if it flips.
Eigenvector
A nonzero vector whose direction a matrix doesn't change: $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$.
Eigenvalue
The stretch factor $\lambda$ for an eigenvector.
Power iteration
Repeatedly multiplying by $A$ to find the top eigenvector.
SVD
$A = U\Sigma V^\top$: any matrix as rotate, stretch, rotate.
PCA
Finding the directions of greatest variation in data.
