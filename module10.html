Probabilistic Modeling, Parameter Estimation & Graphical Models

Everything comes together: fitting a model means choosing the parameters that make your data most probable, and a prior belief is what keeps the model from overreacting.

📊 Intermediate⏱ ~2.5 hours🧮 Needs: Modules 4, 7, 8, 9

Lesson overview

Earlier modules treated regression as geometry (a projection) and as optimization (walking downhill). This module shows the third view: probability. Once you see that "least squares" is really "assume Gaussian noise and pick the most likely parameters", you can design models for any kind of data by choosing what kind of randomness to assume.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Find a maximum-likelihood estimate for a coin from data.
  • Add a prior and compute a MAP estimate.
  • Show why Gaussian noise leads to least squares.
  • Explain ridge regression as a prior belief that parameters are small.
  • Read a simple graphical model and compute a probability with it.
You needBayes and the product rule (Module 7); the Gaussian (Module 8); setting a derivative to zero (Module 9).
This unlocksLogistic regression, Bayesian methods, and modern generative AI, which is all built on likelihoods.

Maximum likelihood: which setting best explains the data?

≈ 25 min

You flip a coin 10 times and get 7 heads. If the coin's chance of heads is $p$, the probability of seeing exactly this sequence is $p^7(1 - p)^3$ (product rule: 7 heads and 3 tails, each flip independent). That expression, read as a function of $p$, is the likelihood. The maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) is the $p$ that makes it largest.

Worked example · try a few values, then solve exactly
  1. $p = 0.5:\; 0.5^7 \times 0.5^3 \approx 0.00098$
    A fair coin can produce 7 heads, but it's not the best explanation.
  2. $p = 0.7:\; 0.7^7 \times 0.3^3 \approx 0.00222$
    More than twice as likely.
  3. $p = 0.9:\; 0.9^7 \times 0.1^3 \approx 0.00048$
    Too extreme: 3 tails would be surprising.
  4. Log-likelihood: $\;7\ln p + 3\ln(1 - p)$
    Taking the log turns products into sums (easier to differentiate) and doesn't move the peak.
  5. Derivative $= \dfrac{7}{p} - \dfrac{3}{1 - p} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 7(1 - p) = 3p \;\Rightarrow\; p = 0.7$
    Set the slope to zero (Module 9) and solve with algebra.
MLE: $\hat{p} = 7/10 = 0.7$, the fraction of heads. The math confirms the common-sense answer.
In real life

A basketball player makes 7 of 10 free throws; the MLE of their skill is 70%. An online ad gets 30 clicks from 1,000 views; the MLE of its click-through rate is 3%. Most models you'll train, including large AI models, are fit by maximizing likelihood: "pick the settings under which the data we saw would be least surprising."

Likelihood asks: "If this were the truth, how unsurprising would my data be?"

Adding prior knowledge: MAP estimation

≈ 20 min

MLE trusts the data completely. With only 10 flips, that can be risky. MAP (maximum a posteriori) estimation uses Bayes' theorem (Module 7) to combine the likelihood with a prior, what you believed before seeing data.

Worked example · a mild belief that coins are usually fair

A simple way to encode "probably near 50/50" is to pretend you already saw 1 extra head and 1 extra tail before flipping.

  1. Heads: $7 + 1 = 8$; total: $10 + 2 = 12$
    Add the imaginary flips from the prior.
  2. $\hat{p}_{\text{MAP}} = 8/12 \approx 0.667$
    Pulled slightly from 0.7 toward 0.5.
  3. After 1,000 flips with 700 heads: $701/1002 \approx 0.6996$
    With lots of data, the prior barely matters.
The prior matters a lot when data is scarce and fades as data grows.
In real life

A new restaurant with one 5-star review isn't really better than one averaging 4.6 stars over 2,000 reviews. Sensible rating systems blend each item's reviews with a prior (the average across all restaurants) so that a single lucky review can't push a newcomer to the top. Sports analysts do the same for a rookie who starts the season 4-for-4.

MAP = data + common sense. Little data leans on the prior; lots of data overrides it.

Why least squares? Because of Gaussian noise

≈ 25 min

Assume each data point is the true line plus Gaussian noise (Module 8): $y_n = \theta x_n + \text{noise}$, with noise $\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$. Let's find the most likely $\theta$.

Worked example · from likelihood to squared error
  1. $p(y_n \mid \theta) \propto e^{-\frac{(y_n - \theta x_n)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$
    The Gaussian density, centered on the line's prediction. ($\propto$ means "proportional to": we drop constants that don't depend on $\theta$.)
  2. $\text{Likelihood} = \prod_n p(y_n \mid \theta)$
    Independent points multiply (product rule).
  3. $\ln(\text{Likelihood}) = -\dfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_n (y_n - \theta x_n)^2 + \text{const}$
    The log turns the product into a sum, and $\ln e^{a} = a$.
  4. Maximize log-likelihood $\iff$ minimize $\sum_n (y_n - \theta x_n)^2$
    The minus sign flips "biggest" into "smallest".
Least squares is maximum likelihood with Gaussian noise. That's why squared error is the default.
In real life

Your phone's GPS gives slightly different readings each second, scattered around your true position like a bell curve. Averaging them (which is the least-squares answer) gives the most likely true position. If you instead expected occasional wild readings, such as from tall buildings reflecting the signal, a different noise assumption gives a different, more robust error measure.

Your choice of error measure is secretly your assumption about the noise.

Ridge regression is a prior belief

≈ 20 min

Add a Gaussian prior that says "$\theta$ is probably small": $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, b^2)$. Repeating the steps above for MAP adds one term:

$$\text{minimize}\quad \sum_n (y_n - \theta x_n)^2 + \lambda\,\theta^2, \qquad \lambda = \frac{\sigma^2}{b^2}.$$

That's ridge regression. The penalty $\lambda\theta^2$ is the prior in disguise.

Worked example · line through the origin, points $(1, 1)$, $(2, 2)$, $(3, 2)$
  1. $\sum x_n y_n = 1 + 4 + 6 = 11, \quad \sum x_n^2 = 1 + 4 + 9 = 14$
    The two sums we need.
  2. Least squares: $\theta = \dfrac{\sum x y}{\sum x^2} = \dfrac{11}{14} \approx 0.786$
    Set the derivative of $\sum(y - \theta x)^2$ to zero: $-2\sum x(y - \theta x) = 0$.
  3. Ridge with $\lambda = 1$: $\theta = \dfrac{\sum x y}{\sum x^2 + \lambda} = \dfrac{11}{15} \approx 0.733$
    Same derivative plus $2\lambda\theta$ from the penalty. The $\lambda$ lands in the denominator.
The prior shrinks the slope toward 0. A bigger $\lambda$ means a stronger belief, and more shrinking.
In real life

If a stock went up three days in a row, a sensible forecast doesn't predict it will rise forever at that rate. Shrinking estimates toward zero protects against overfitting: memorizing noise in a small dataset instead of learning the real pattern. Nearly every production ML model uses some form of this.

Regularization = a prior that says "don't get carried away".

Graphical models: drawing what causes what

≈ 30 min

A graphical model is a diagram of how variables depend on each other. Arrows point from a variable to the things it directly influences. The diagram tells you how to break a complicated probability into small pieces.

The diagram says the joint probability factors as $p(R, S, W) = p(R)\,p(S)\,p(W \mid R, S)$. Use $p(R) = 0.2$, $p(S) = 0.1$, and grass is wet with probability 0.99 if both happen, 0.9 if only rain, 0.8 if only the sprinkler, and 0 if neither.

Worked example · the grass is wet. Did it rain?
  1. Rain & sprinkler: $0.2 \times 0.1 \times 0.99 = 0.0198$
    Each case uses the factorization: multiply along the diagram.
  2. Rain only: $0.2 \times 0.9 \times 0.9 = 0.162$
    $p(\text{no sprinkler}) = 0.9$.
  3. Sprinkler only: $0.8 \times 0.1 \times 0.8 = 0.064$
    $p(\text{no rain}) = 0.8$.
  4. $p(W) = 0.0198 + 0.162 + 0.064 + 0 = 0.2458$
    Add every way the grass can be wet.
  5. $p(R \mid W) = \dfrac{0.0198 + 0.162}{0.2458} \approx 0.74$
    Bayes: the share of wet-grass cases that involve rain.
  6. Now learn the sprinkler was on: $p(R \mid W, S) = \dfrac{0.2 \times 0.99}{0.2 \times 0.99 + 0.8 \times 0.8} \approx 0.24$
    The sprinkler already explains the wet grass, so rain becomes much less likely.
Wet grass makes rain likely (74%), but finding the sprinkler on drops it to 24%. This is called explaining away.
In real life

Your car won't start. A dead battery and an empty tank are both possible causes. If you then notice the fuel gauge reads empty, a dead battery suddenly seems less likely, even though the gauge says nothing about the battery directly. Medical diagnosis systems, spam filters, and recommendation engines use diagrams like this to reason about hidden causes from visible symptoms.

Arrows show what influences what; multiply along them; when one cause is confirmed, the others fade.
Careful

Arrows in a graphical model describe how the probabilities are organized. Whether they represent real causes is a separate question that data alone usually can't settle.

Key terms

Likelihood
The probability of the observed data, viewed as a function of the parameters.
MLE
The parameters that make the observed data most probable.
Prior
What you believe about parameters before seeing data.
MAP
The parameters that maximize likelihood × prior.
Ridge regression
Least squares plus $\lambda\theta^2$; equivalent to a Gaussian prior.
Overfitting
Fitting noise in the training data instead of the real pattern.
Graphical model
A diagram showing which variables directly depend on which.
