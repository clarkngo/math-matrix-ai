Orthonormal Basis & Orthogonal Projections

A projection finds the closest point. That one idea is secretly what linear regression does.

📊 Intermediate⏱ ~2 hours🧮 Needs: Module 3
Overview Projecting onto a Line Projection Matrices Gram–Schmidt Regression = Projection Review

Lesson overview

Imagine shining a light straight down onto a line: a vector's shadow on that line is its projection. The shadow is the closest point on the line to the tip of the vector, and the gap between them is perpendicular to the line. When data can't be fit perfectly, "closest possible" is exactly what we want, and that's why least-squares regression is a projection.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Project a vector onto a line and check that the leftover piece is perpendicular.
  • Build a projection matrix and explain why applying it twice changes nothing.
  • Turn two vectors into perpendicular unit vectors with Gram–Schmidt.
  • Explain least-squares regression as projecting the data onto the model's possibilities.
You needDot products, lengths, and orthogonality (Module 3); the best-fit line (Module 1).
This unlocksQR decomposition, PCA (Module 5), and Module 10's view of regression.

Projecting onto a line: finding the shadow

≈ 25 min

To project $\mathbf{b}$ onto the line through $\mathbf{a}$, use

$$\text{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\,\mathbf{b} = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}}\;\mathbf{a}.$$

The fraction is just a number: how many copies of $\mathbf{a}$ the shadow is.

Worked example · project $\mathbf{b} = (2, 3)$ onto $\mathbf{a} = (1, 1)$
  1. $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 1\cdot2 + 1\cdot3 = 5$
    How much $\mathbf{b}$ lines up with $\mathbf{a}$.
  2. $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a} = 1 + 1 = 2$
    The length of $\mathbf{a}$, squared. Dividing by it removes the effect of $\mathbf{a}$'s own size.
  3. $\text{proj} = \tfrac{5}{2}(1, 1) = (2.5, 2.5)$
    2.5 copies of $\mathbf{a}$.
  4. Leftover: $\mathbf{b} - \text{proj} = (2 - 2.5,\; 3 - 2.5) = (-0.5, 0.5)$
    The part of $\mathbf{b}$ the line can't capture. It's called the residual.
  5. Check: $(-0.5, 0.5)\cdot(1, 1) = -0.5 + 0.5 = 0$ ✓
    The residual is perpendicular to the line. This always happens, and it's why the shadow is the closest point.
$\mathbf{b} = \underbrace{(2.5, 2.5)}_{\text{along the line}} + \underbrace{(-0.5, 0.5)}_{\text{perpendicular}}$
You already know this

The shortest path from a point to a line is the perpendicular one. You may have used that in geometry to find the distance from a point to a line.

In real life

A pilot heading northeast, direction $(1, 1)$, flies through a wind of $(2, 3)$ km/h. How much of that wind is a tailwind? It's the length of the projection: $\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 3.54$ km/h pushing the plane forward; the rest is a crosswind. Engineers split forces the same way: the part of gravity pulling a car down a ramp is a projection.

Projection = the shadow; residual = the part that sticks straight out.
Try it yourself (click to reveal the answer)
Project $(4, 2)$ onto $(1, 0)$, the horizontal axis.
$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 4$, $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a} = 1$, so the projection is $4(1, 0) = (4, 0)$. The shadow on the floor just drops the height, as you'd expect.

Projection matrices: doing it twice changes nothing

≈ 15 min

You can package "project onto $\mathbf{a}$" as a matrix $P = \dfrac{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^\top}{\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{a}}$, then project anything with $P\mathbf{b}$.

Worked example · $P$ for $\mathbf{a} = (1, 1)$
  1. $\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^\top = \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$
    Column times row gives a matrix (every pair of entries multiplied).
  2. $P = \tfrac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0.5&0.5\\0.5&0.5\end{bmatrix}$
    Divide by $\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{a} = 2$.
  3. $P\begin{bmatrix}2\\3\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0.5(2) + 0.5(3)\\0.5(2) + 0.5(3)\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2.5\\2.5\end{bmatrix}$ ✓
    Same answer as before.
  4. $P^2 = P$
    Projecting a shadow again gives the same shadow: it's already on the line. (Multiply it out: each entry is $0.5\cdot0.5 + 0.5\cdot0.5 = 0.5$.)
In real life

A photo filter that converts color to grayscale is a projection: applying it to an already-gray photo does nothing. Flattening a 3D scene onto your 2D screen in a video game is also a projection.

Project once and you're done: $P^2 = P$.

Gram–Schmidt: making perpendicular unit vectors

≈ 25 min

Perpendicular vectors of length 1 are the easiest basis to work with. Such a basis is called orthonormal. Gram–Schmidt turns any independent vectors into one: keep the first vector's direction, then remove from each next vector its shadow on the earlier ones.

Worked example · start with $\mathbf{v}_1 = (3, 4)$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (1, 0)$
  1. $\mathbf{u}_1 = \dfrac{(3, 4)}{5} = (0.6, 0.8)$
    Divide by the length (5) to get a unit vector in the same direction.
  2. Shadow of $\mathbf{v}_2$ on $\mathbf{u}_1$: $(\mathbf{v}_2\cdot\mathbf{u}_1)\,\mathbf{u}_1 = 0.6\,(0.6, 0.8) = (0.36, 0.48)$
    Because $\mathbf{u}_1$ has length 1, the projection formula simplifies (no dividing).
  3. $\mathbf{w} = (1, 0) - (0.36, 0.48) = (0.64, -0.48)$
    Remove the shadow; what's left is perpendicular to $\mathbf{u}_1$.
  4. $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert = \sqrt{0.64^2 + 0.48^2} = 0.8 \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{u}_2 = (0.8, -0.6)$
    Scale it to length 1.
  5. Check: $\mathbf{u}_1\cdot\mathbf{u}_2 = 0.48 - 0.48 = 0$ ✓
    Perpendicular, and both have length 1.
In real life

A phone's motion sensors are never mounted perfectly square. Software straightens the measured axes into a clean perpendicular set, the same idea as Gram–Schmidt, so "tilt forward" and "tilt sideways" don't bleed into each other. Statistics software solves regression this way too (a QR decomposition), because it's more numerically stable than computing $X^\top X$.

Keep the first direction; strip each new one of its shadows; resize to length 1.

Machine learning: least squares is a projection

≈ 25 min

Back to the best-fit line from Module 1: points $(1, 1)$, $(2, 2)$, $(3, 2)$ and the line $y = 0.667 + 0.5x$. Look at the line's predictions as one vector:

Worked example · predictions, residuals, and the perpendicular check
  1. $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = (0.667 + 0.5,\; 0.667 + 1,\; 0.667 + 1.5) = (1.167, 1.667, 2.167)$
    Predicted $y$ at $x = 1, 2, 3$.
  2. $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}} = (1 - 1.167,\; 2 - 1.667,\; 2 - 2.167) = (-0.167, 0.333, -0.167)$
    The errors (residuals).
  3. $(1, 1, 1)\cdot\mathbf{r} = -0.167 + 0.333 - 0.167 = 0$
    Residual is perpendicular to the first column of $X$ (the 1s).
  4. $(1, 2, 3)\cdot\mathbf{r} = -0.167 + 0.667 - 0.5 = 0$
    …and to the second column (the $x$ values).
The errors are perpendicular to everything the model can produce. So $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is the projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto the model's possibilities: the closest the model can get.

Writing "residual perpendicular to every column of $X$" as an equation gives $X^\top(\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathbf{0}$. Rearrange it and you get Module 1's normal equations, $X^\top X\boldsymbol\theta = X^\top\mathbf{y}$. That's where they come from.

In real life

Any trend line in a spreadsheet chart (sales by month, temperature by year, rent by square footage) is computed this way. The leftover errors being "perpendicular" means there's no straight-line pattern left in them that the model could still capture.

The best fit is the shadow of the data on what the model can do.

Key terms

Projection
The closest point on a line or subspace; the "shadow" of a vector.
Residual
What's left after projecting: $\mathbf{b} - \text{proj}\,\mathbf{b}$. Always perpendicular.
Orthonormal
Vectors that are mutually perpendicular and each of length 1.
Gram–Schmidt
A procedure that turns independent vectors into an orthonormal set.
Least squares
Choosing parameters to minimize the sum of squared residuals.
