Module 1 · Linear Algebra

Systems of Linear Equations & Matrix Operations

You've solved two equations with two unknowns. A matrix is just a tidy way to write that problem, and it scales to thousands of unknowns.

📊 Beginner⏱ ~2 hours🧮 Needs: Module 0
Overview Two Equations Writing It as a Matrix Elimination Multiplying Matrices The Inverse ML: Fitting a Line Review

Lesson overview

A linear equation is one where variables are only multiplied by numbers and added, with no $x^2$, no $xy$, no $\sqrt{x}$. "$2x + y = 5$" is linear. A system is several of them that must all be true at once. Machine learning is full of these: fitting a straight line to data turns into exactly this kind of system.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Solve a 2×2 and a 3×3 system by elimination.
  • Rewrite any linear system as $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$.
  • Multiply a matrix by a vector, and two matrices together.
  • Compute the inverse of a 2×2 matrix and know when it doesn't exist.
  • Set up and solve the "best-fit line" problem as a linear system.
You needSolving $2x + y = 5$ and $x - y = 1$ together. Vectors from Module 0.
This unlocksRank and spaces (Module 2), projections (Module 4), and linear regression.
Part A · From algebra to matrices
✖️

Start with what you know: two equations, two unknowns

≈ 15 min
Worked example · solve by elimination $$\begin{aligned} 2x + y &= 5 \\ x - y &= 1 \end{aligned}$$
  1. $(2x + y) + (x - y) = 5 + 1 \;\Rightarrow\; 3x = 6$
    Add the equations. The $+y$ and $-y$ cancel, which is the whole point of elimination.
  2. $x = 2$
    Divide both sides by 3.
  3. $2 - y = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; y = 1$
    Put $x = 2$ back into the second equation.
  4. Check: $2(2) + 1 = 5$ ✓ and $2 - 1 = 1$ ✓
    Always check in both original equations.
Solution: $x = 2,\; y = 1$
In real life

At a coffee shop, 2 lattes and 1 muffin cost \$11, and a latte costs \$1 more than a muffin. What does each cost? Let $L$ = latte price and $M$ = muffin price:

$$2L + M = 11, \qquad L - M = 1.$$

Add the two equations: $3L = 12$, so $L = 4$ and $M = 3$. Any time you have several clues about several unknown amounts (prices, quantities, mixtures), you're solving a linear system.

One unknown needs one clue. Two unknowns need two clues.

Each equation is a line on a graph. The solution is where the lines cross. That picture tells you there are only three things that can happen:

Lines…ExampleNumber of solutions
cross once$2x + y = 5,\; x - y = 1$Exactly one
are parallel$x + y = 2,\; x + y = 5$None (the same thing can't equal 2 and 5)
are the same line$x + y = 2,\; 2x + 2y = 4$Infinitely many (the second is just the first times 2)

The same three outcomes hold for any number of equations and unknowns. With 3 unknowns, each equation is a flat plane in 3D instead of a line.

🧱

Writing the system as a matrix

≈ 20 min

Notice that the letters $x$ and $y$ don't really matter; only the numbers in front of them (the coefficients) and the right-hand sides do. So we pull the numbers out into a grid called a matrix:

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}} \qquad\text{or just}\qquad A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}.$$

Row 1 of $A$ holds the coefficients of equation 1; row 2 holds equation 2. A matrix with 2 rows and 2 columns is called "2×2" (rows first).

How matrix × vector works

To multiply $A\mathbf{x}$: for each row of $A$, multiply its entries by the matching entries of $\mathbf{x}$ and add. That's exactly "plug $x$ and $y$ into the left side of each equation".

Worked example · check the solution with a matrix $$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
  1. Row 1: $2\cdot2 + 1\cdot1 = 5$
    First row times the vector, entry by entry, then add.
  2. Row 2: $1\cdot2 + (-1)\cdot1 = 1$
    Second row, same pattern.
$A\mathbf{x} = (5, 1) = \mathbf{b}$ ✓, so $(2, 1)$ solves the system.
Another way to see it

A matrix is also a machine that moves points. Feed it the point $(2, 1)$ and it outputs $(5, 1)$. Solving $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ asks: "which input point does the machine send to $\mathbf{b}$?" Module 5 builds on this picture.

In real life

A spreadsheet of orders is a matrix. Each row is an order; the columns count lattes and muffins. Multiply by the price vector and you get every customer's bill at once:

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{orders}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{prices}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2(4) + 1(3) \\ 1(4) + 3(3) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 11 \\ 13 \end{bmatrix} \text{ dollars}.$$

The register at a busy store does this for thousands of orders, and a recommendation engine does it for millions of users.

Rows are orders, the vector is the price list, and $A\mathbf{x}$ is the stack of bills.
Try it yourself (click to reveal the answer)
Write $3x - 2y = 4$ and $x + 5y = -1$ as $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$. Then compute $A$ times $(1, 1)$.
$A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{b} = (4, -1)$. $A(1,1) = (3 - 2,\; 1 + 5) = (1, 6)$, which isn't $\mathbf{b}$, so $(1, 1)$ is not the solution.

Gaussian elimination: the same trick, organized

≈ 25 min

With three unknowns, you use the same move as before (subtract a multiple of one equation from another to cancel a variable) but you do it in a fixed order. This systematic version is called Gaussian elimination, and it's what computers do.

Worked example · a 3×3 system $$\begin{aligned} x + y + z &= 6 &\text{(R1)}\\ 2y + 5z &= -4 &\text{(R2)}\\ 2x + 5y - z &= 27 &\text{(R3)} \end{aligned}$$
  1. R3 − 2·R1: $\;(2x + 5y - z) - 2(x + y + z) = 27 - 12$
    Goal: remove $x$ from R3. R1 has $1x$, R3 has $2x$, so subtract 2 copies of R1.
  2. $3y - 3z = 15 \;\Rightarrow\; y - z = 5$
    Simplify, then divide by 3. Now two equations (this and R2) have only $y$ and $z$.
  3. $y = 5 + z$
    Solve for $y$ so we can substitute.
  4. $2(5 + z) + 5z = -4 \;\Rightarrow\; 10 + 7z = -4 \;\Rightarrow\; z = -2$
    Substitute into R2. Now it's a one-variable equation.
  5. $y = 5 + (-2) = 3$
    Work backwards ("back-substitution").
  6. $x = 6 - y - z = 6 - 3 + 2 = 5$
    Use R1 to get the last unknown.
Solution: $(x, y, z) = (5, 3, -2)$. Check R3: $2(5) + 5(3) - (-2) = 10 + 15 + 2 = 27$ ✓
You already know this

This is the "elimination method" from algebra class, done in a strict order: clear $x$ from every row below the first, then $y$ from every row below the second, and so on, then solve from the bottom up.

In real life
  • Meal planning apps choose amounts of 3 foods to hit 3 targets (protein, carbs, fat): 3 equations, 3 unknowns.
  • Chemistry: balancing a reaction like $\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \to \text{H}_2\text{O}$ means one equation per element.
  • Traffic engineers write one equation per intersection (cars in = cars out) and solve for flows on every street.
Clear one variable at a time going down, then solve going back up. 
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[1, 1, 1], [0, 2, 5], [2, 5, -1]])
b = np.array([6, -4, 27])
print(np.linalg.solve(A, b))   # [ 5.  3. -2.]

Multiplying two matrices

≈ 20 min

To multiply $AB$, take each row of $A$ and each column of $B$, multiply matching entries, and add. The result goes in that row-and-column spot.

Worked example · $AB$ with $A = \begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$
  1. Top-left: row 1 of $A$ · column 1 of $B$ $= 1\cdot0 + 2\cdot1 = 2$
    Row of the first, column of the second.
  2. Top-right: row 1 · column 2 $= 1\cdot1 + 2\cdot0 = 1$
  3. Bottom-left: row 2 · column 1 $= 3\cdot0 + 4\cdot1 = 4$
  4. Bottom-right: row 2 · column 2 $= 3\cdot1 + 4\cdot0 = 3$
$AB = \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\4&3\end{bmatrix}$, but $BA = \begin{bmatrix}3&4\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$. They're different!

Order matters. With numbers, $3 \times 5 = 5 \times 3$. With matrices, $AB$ and $BA$ are usually different. Here $B$ swaps things: $AB$ swapped the columns of $A$, while $BA$ swapped the rows. Think of matrices as actions: "put on socks, then shoes" is not the same as "shoes, then socks".

In real life

Open a photo on your phone and try: rotate 90°, then flip horizontally. Undo, then flip first, then rotate. The two results face different directions. Each edit is a matrix, and doing two edits is multiplying their matrices, so order matters. Graphics cards in games multiply these "move, rotate, scale" matrices millions of times per second.

Socks then shoes is not shoes then socks: $AB \ne BA$.

The transpose

The transpose $A^\top$ flips a matrix over its diagonal, so rows become columns: $\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix}^\top = \begin{bmatrix}1&3\\2&4\end{bmatrix}$. You'll see it constantly in ML formulas.

Try it yourself (click to reveal the answer)
Compute $\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\2&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$.
Top row: $(1\cdot3 + 0\cdot0,\; 1\cdot1 + 0\cdot2) = (3, 1)$. Bottom row: $(2\cdot3 + 1\cdot0,\; 2\cdot1 + 1\cdot2) = (6, 4)$. Result: $\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\6&4\end{bmatrix}$.

The inverse: "dividing" by a matrix

≈ 15 min

To solve $5x = 10$ you multiply both sides by $\frac{1}{5}$. For $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ the matching idea is the inverse $A^{-1}$, the matrix that undoes $A$. Then $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$.

For a 2×2 matrix there's a formula:

$$\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$$
Worked example · invert $A = \begin{bmatrix}4&7\\2&6\end{bmatrix}$
  1. $ad - bc = 4\cdot6 - 7\cdot2 = 24 - 14 = 10$
    This number is the determinant. If it's 0, stop: there is no inverse.
  2. $\begin{bmatrix} 6 & -7 \\ -2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$
    Swap $a$ and $d$; flip the signs of $b$ and $c$.
  3. $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{10}\begin{bmatrix} 6 & -7 \\ -2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & -0.7 \\ -0.2 & 0.4 \end{bmatrix}$
    Divide every entry by the determinant.
In real life

The inverse is an undo button. A simple secret code (the Hill cipher) scrambles a message by multiplying by a matrix and unscrambles it with the inverse. Encode the message $(2, 1)$ with $A$ from the example:

$$A\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4(2) + 7(1)\\2(2) + 6(1)\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}15\\10\end{bmatrix}, \qquad A^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}15\\10\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0.6(15) - 0.7(10)\\-0.2(15) + 0.4(10)\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}.$$

If the determinant were 0, two different messages could scramble to the same code, and you could never tell them apart.

Inverse = undo. Determinant 0 = information was destroyed, so there's nothing to undo.
When there's no inverse

$\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\2&2\end{bmatrix}$ has determinant $1\cdot2 - 1\cdot2 = 0$. Its two rows say the same thing (the second is twice the first), which is the "same line" case from earlier: infinitely many solutions or none, never exactly one. In practice, computers solve systems by elimination rather than computing $A^{-1}$, because it's faster and more accurate.

Machine learning: fitting a line to data

≈ 25 min

Here's where this pays off. Suppose you have three data points: $(1, 1)$, $(2, 2)$, $(3, 2)$, and you want the line $y = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x$ that fits them best. No single line passes through all three, so we want the one with the smallest total squared error (Module 4 explains why this is the right choice).

Put the inputs into a design matrix $X$ (a column of 1s for the intercept, then the $x$ values) and the outputs into $\mathbf{y}$:

$$X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

The best-fit parameters solve a 2×2 system called the normal equations: $X^\top X\,\boldsymbol\theta = X^\top\mathbf{y}$.

Worked example · best-fit line, step by step
  1. $X^\top X = \begin{bmatrix} 1+1+1 & 1+2+3 \\ 1+2+3 & 1+4+9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}$
    Row-times-column of $X^\top$ and $X$. The entries are the count of points, $\sum x$, and $\sum x^2$.
  2. $X^\top \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 1+2+2 \\ 1\cdot1 + 2\cdot2 + 3\cdot2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}$
    $\sum y$ and $\sum xy$.
  3. $\det = 3\cdot14 - 6\cdot6 = 6$
    Not zero, so there's exactly one best line.
  4. $\boldsymbol\theta = \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 14 & -6 \\ -6 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 70 - 66 \\ -30 + 33 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.667 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix}$
    Use the 2×2 inverse formula from the previous section.
Best-fit line: $y = 0.667 + 0.5x$
In real life

Home-value estimates on real-estate sites start from this idea: fit price against size (and dozens of other features) across thousands of past sales. Same with predicting delivery time from distance, or a store's sales from ad spend. If the fitted line is $\text{price} = 50 + 0.2 \times \text{sq ft}$ (in thousands), the slope says each extra square foot adds about \$200.

Best-fit line = solve one small system, $X^\top X\boldsymbol\theta = X^\top\mathbf{y}$.
Why this matters for ML

This is linear regression, the starting point of machine learning. With 10,000 data points and 50 features, $X$ is 10,000×50, but $X^\top X$ is still only 50×50, and you solve it exactly the same way.

X = np.array([[1, 1], [1, 2], [1, 3]])
y = np.array([1, 2, 2])
theta = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)
print(theta)   # [0.6667 0.5   ]

Key terms

Matrix
A rectangular grid of numbers; m×n means m rows and n columns.
Gaussian elimination
Solving a system by canceling variables in a fixed order, then back-substituting.
Transpose $A^\top$
The matrix with rows and columns swapped.
Inverse $A^{-1}$
The matrix that undoes $A$; exists only when the determinant isn't 0.
Determinant
For 2×2, $ad - bc$. Zero means no unique solution.
Normal equations
$X^\top X\boldsymbol\theta = X^\top \mathbf{y}$, the system that gives the best-fit line.
