Module 9 · Optimization

Continuous Optimization & Gradient Descent

Training a model is walking downhill on its error surface. Step too timidly and you crawl; step too boldly and you fly off the mountain.

📊 Intermediate⏱ ~2 hours🧮 Needs: Modules 5 and 6
Overview One Step at a Time Step Size Narrow Valleys Convexity Constraints Review

Lesson overview

In algebra you found the minimum of a parabola by completing the square. Real models have millions of parameters and no formula for the answer, so instead we start somewhere and repeatedly step downhill using the gradient (Module 6). That's gradient descent, and it trains nearly every model in use today.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Run several steps of gradient descent by hand.
  • Find the largest learning rate that still converges, and explain why.
  • Explain why narrow valleys slow training and how feature scaling helps.
  • Tell convex problems (one valley) from non-convex ones (many).
  • Solve a simple constrained problem with a Lagrange multiplier.

Gradient descent, one step at a time

≈ 20 min

The rule: new position = old position − (step size) × (slope).

$$x_{k+1} = x_k - \eta\, f'(x_k)$$

The step size $\eta$ (eta) is called the learning rate.

Worked example · minimize $f(x) = (x - 3)^2$ from $x_0 = 0$ with $\eta = 0.1$

The slope is $f'(x) = 2(x - 3)$. (By algebra we already know the answer is $x = 3$, so we can check.)

  1. $x_1 = 0 - 0.1 \times 2(0 - 3) = 0 + 0.6 = 0.6$
    The slope at 0 is $-6$ (going downhill to the right), so we move right.
  2. $x_2 = 0.6 - 0.1 \times 2(0.6 - 3) = 0.6 + 0.48 = 1.08$
    The slope is gentler now ($-4.8$), so the step is smaller.
  3. $x_3 = 1.08 + 0.384 = 1.464$
    Steps keep shrinking as the ground flattens.
  4. Distance to 3: $\;3 \to 2.4 \to 1.92 \to 1.536$
    Each step multiplies the remaining distance by $0.8$.
Slow and steady: the error shrinks by 20% every step and approaches $x = 3$.
In real life

You're on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the valley floor. You can't see the bottom, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a step downhill, feel again, and step again. On steep ground you take bigger strides; near the bottom, smaller ones. That's gradient descent exactly: the model can't "see" the best parameters, only the local slope of its error.

Feel the slope, step downhill, repeat.

Choosing the step size

≈ 25 min

Let $e = x - 3$ be the error. One step gives $e_{\text{new}} = e - \eta \cdot 2e = (1 - 2\eta)\,e$. Everything depends on that multiplier $(1 - 2\eta)$.

$\eta$Multiplier $1 - 2\eta$What happens
0.10.8Shrinks 20% per step: steady
0.50Lands exactly on 3 in one step
0.9−0.8Overshoots back and forth, but still shrinks (zig-zag)
1.1−1.2Overshoots by more each time: diverges
Worked example · $\eta = 1.1$ blows up
  1. $x_1 = 0 - 1.1 \times (-6) = 6.6$
    Jumped right past 3, now 3.6 away.
  2. $x_2 = 6.6 - 1.1 \times 7.2 = -1.32$
    Overshot the other way, now 4.32 away.
  3. $x_3 = 8.184$
    5.18 away. Each step makes it worse.
The rule: we need $|1 - 2\eta| < 1$, so $0 < \eta < 1$. In general $\eta < 2 / (\text{curvature})$, and here the curvature is $f''(x) = 2$.
In real life

A new driver parallel parking turns the wheel too far, overcorrects, then overcorrects again, zig-zagging and never settling. A thermostat that blasts full heat whenever it's 1 degree cold does the same. Every control system, from cruise control to a drone's stabilizer, has to pick a correction size that's strong enough to make progress but gentle enough not to overshoot.

Too small: crawling. Too big: overshooting and spiraling out.

For $f(x) = 5x^2$, what's the largest learning rate that still converges?
Curvature $f''(x) = 10$, so $\eta < 2/10 = 0.2$. (Check: $f'(x) = 10x$, multiplier $1 - 10\eta$, and $|1 - 10\eta| < 1$ means $\eta < 0.2$.)

Narrow valleys: why some problems are slow

≈ 25 min

With two parameters, a surface can be steep in one direction and nearly flat in the other, like a long narrow canyon. Take $f(x, y) = \tfrac{1}{2}(x^2 + 10y^2)$: curvature 1 along $x$, and 10 along $y$.

Worked example · one learning rate, two directions
  1. $x$ multiplier: $1 - \eta$; $\quad y$ multiplier: $1 - 10\eta$
    Same reasoning as Section 2, once per direction.
  2. Stability needs $|1 - 10\eta| < 1 \Rightarrow \eta < 0.2$
    The steep direction sets the speed limit.
  3. With $\eta = 0.15$: $x$ multiplier $0.85$, $y$ multiplier $-0.5$
    $y$ zig-zags and settles fast; $x$ creeps along, keeping 85% of its error every step.
The ratio of steepest to flattest curvature (here 10) is the condition number $\kappa$. These curvatures are the eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix (Module 5). Larger $\kappa$ means slower training.

Fix: put features on similar scales. If one feature is "price in dollars" (thousands) and another is "number of rooms" (single digits), the error surface is a stretched canyon. Converting both to z-scores (Module 8) rounds it into a bowl. Momentum, which keeps some of the previous step's direction like a rolling ball, also helps by smoothing out the zig-zag.

In real life

Walking down a steep, narrow gorge whose floor slopes gently toward the exit, you'd waste your energy bouncing between the steep walls while barely moving toward the exit. A heavy bowling ball rolling down the same gorge (momentum) evens out the side-to-side bouncing and keeps rolling forward.

The steepest direction sets the speed limit; the flattest direction sets how long the trip takes.

Convex vs. non-convex: one valley or many?

≈ 15 min

A function is convex if it's shaped like a bowl: any straight line between two points on the curve stays above the curve. A convex function has only one valley, so walking downhill always finds the best answer.

Worked example · $f(x) = x^4 - 3x^2$ has two valleys
  1. $f'(x) = 4x^3 - 6x = 2x(2x^2 - 3)$
    Power rule, then factor.
  2. $f'(x) = 0$ at $x = 0$ and $x = \pm\sqrt{1.5} \approx \pm1.22$
    Flat points: a hilltop at 0, and two valley floors.
  3. Start at $x = 0.5$: you roll to $+1.22$. Start at $x = -0.5$: you roll to $-1.22$.
    Where you end up depends on where you start.
Not convex. Here both valleys happen to be equally deep; in general, gradient descent can get stuck in a shallower one.
In real life

Hiking down in fog might land you in a small dip halfway down the mountain rather than the true valley floor. Linear regression is convex, so it always finds the best fit. Neural networks are not convex, which is why training them involves tricks like random restarts and noisy steps (stochastic gradient descent) to avoid getting stuck.

Convex = one bowl, so downhill always wins. Non-convex = many dips, so where you start matters.

Constraints: the best answer that follows the rules

≈ 20 min

Sometimes the answer must satisfy a rule, such as a budget. At the best allowed point, the downhill direction of $f$ must point straight into the rule's boundary (otherwise you could slide along the boundary and do better). That means the two gradients line up: $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$, where $\lambda$ is a Lagrange multiplier.

Worked example · minimize $f = x^2 + y^2$ subject to $x + y = 2$
  1. $\nabla f = (2x, 2y), \quad \nabla g = (1, 1)$ where $g = x + y$
    Gradients of the goal and of the rule.
  2. $(2x, 2y) = \lambda(1, 1) \Rightarrow 2x = \lambda = 2y \Rightarrow x = y$
    Set them parallel.
  3. $x + x = 2 \Rightarrow x = y = 1$
    Use the rule to finish.
The point on the line $x + y = 2$ closest to the origin is $(1, 1)$, at distance $\sqrt{2}$. This is the projection from Module 4, found a different way.
In real life

Where should a bus stop on a straight road go so it's closest to your house? Where should you spend a fixed ad budget across two channels for the most sales? Airlines schedule crews and planes the same way: minimize cost, subject to rules about rest hours and aircraft availability.

At the best allowed point, the goal's slope points straight into the fence.

Key terms

Gradient descent
Repeatedly step opposite the gradient: $\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x} - \eta\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$.
Learning rate $\eta$
The step size; must be below $2/(\text{largest curvature})$.
Condition number $\kappa$
Steepest curvature ÷ flattest curvature; large means slow training.
Momentum
Carrying part of the previous step forward to smooth out zig-zags.
Convex
Bowl-shaped; has a single minimum.
Lagrange multiplier
The factor $\lambda$ in $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$ at a constrained optimum.
