Norms, Inner Products & Orthogonality

How long is a vector, how far apart are two of them, and how similar are they? One operation, the dot product, answers all three.

Lesson overview

Machine learning constantly asks "how close is this to that?" Which song is closest to the ones you like? Which past patient is most similar to this one? How far is the prediction from the truth? This module gives you the tools for measuring that: length (a norm), distance, and angle.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Compute the length of a vector and the distance between two points.
  • Compare three ways of measuring length (ℓ1, ℓ2, ℓ∞) and say when each fits.
  • Compute a dot product and use it to find the angle between vectors.
  • Use cosine similarity to compare documents.
  • Recognize orthogonal (perpendicular) vectors.

Length: the Pythagorean theorem in disguise

≈ 15 min

The vector $(3, 4)$ goes 3 across and 4 up. Those two moves form the legs of a right triangle, and the arrow is the hypotenuse. So its length, written $\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert$ and called the norm, comes straight from Pythagoras:

Worked example · length of $(3, 4)$ and distance between two points
  1. $\lVert(3, 4)\rVert = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$
    Square each entry, add, take the square root.
  2. Distance from $(1, 1)$ to $(4, 5)$: subtract first, $(4-1,\; 5-1) = (3, 4)$
    The distance between two points is the length of the arrow from one to the other.
  3. $\lVert(3, 4)\rVert = 5$
    Same arrow as before.
$\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_2 = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \dots + x_n^2}$ works in any number of dimensions.
You already know this

This is the distance formula from algebra, $d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$, written for vectors.

In real life

"As the crow flies" distance is this norm. Your phone's "find nearby restaurants" sorts places by it. In ML, the error of a prediction is often the distance between the predicted vector and the true one.

Length = Pythagoras. Distance = length of the difference.
🚕

Other ways to measure length

≈ 15 min

Straight-line distance isn't the only reasonable measure. For $\mathbf{x} = (3, 4)$:

NormFormulaFor $(3, 4)$Think of it as
$\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_2$$\sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2}$$5$Straight line (a bird)
$\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_1$$|x_1| + |x_2|$$3 + 4 = 7$City blocks (a taxi)
$\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_\infty$$\max(|x_1|, |x_2|)$$\max(3, 4) = 4$The biggest single move (a chess king)
In real life

In Manhattan, a taxi going 3 blocks east and 4 blocks north drives 7 blocks (ℓ1), even though the straight-line distance is 5 (ℓ2). A chess king needs 4 moves to cover the same offset, because it can move diagonally (ℓ∞). Warehouses use ℓ1 to plan picker routes along aisles.

In ML: adding an ℓ1 penalty to a model (called Lasso) pushes many parameters to exactly zero, which picks out a few important features. It's like a strict luggage limit that makes you leave whole items at home instead of packing a little of everything.

ℓ2 is the bird, ℓ1 is the taxi, ℓ∞ is the chess king.

The dot product: multiply and add

≈ 15 min

The dot product (also called the inner product) of two vectors multiplies matching entries and adds the results. It produces a single number.

Worked example · $(1, 2) \cdot (3, 4)$
  1. $1 \times 3 = 3, \quad 2 \times 4 = 8$
    Multiply first with first, second with second.
  2. $3 + 8 = 11$
    Add them up.
$(1, 2) \cdot (3, 4) = 11$. Also written $\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{y}$.

Two facts make it useful:

  • A vector dotted with itself gives its length squared: $(3, 4)\cdot(3, 4) = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5^2$.
  • Each row of a matrix-times-vector product (Module 1) is a dot product.
In real life

A shopping bill is a dot product: quantities $(2, 1, 4)$ dotted with prices $(3, 5, 1)$ is $6 + 5 + 4 = 15$ dollars. A course grade is a dot product of scores and weights: scores $(90, 80)$ with weights $(0.6, 0.4)$ gives $54 + 32 = 86$. Every neuron in a neural network computes a dot product of its inputs and weights.

Dot product = "weighted total": multiply pairs, add them up.

Angles and cosine similarity

≈ 25 min

The dot product also measures how much two vectors point the same way. The formula is

$$\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert}.$$

$\cos\theta = 1$ means same direction, $0$ means perpendicular, $-1$ means opposite.

Worked example · angle between $(1, 2)$ and $(3, 4)$
  1. $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y} = 11$
    From the previous section.
  2. $\lVert(1, 2)\rVert = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.236, \quad \lVert(3, 4)\rVert = 5$
    Lengths by Pythagoras.
  3. $\cos\theta = \dfrac{11}{2.236 \times 5} = \dfrac{11}{11.18} \approx 0.984$
    Very close to 1: nearly the same direction.
  4. $\theta \approx 10.3°$
    Use $\cos^{-1}$ on a calculator.

The formula can never give more than 1, because $|\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}| \le \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert$ always (the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality). Here $11 \le 11.18$ ✓.

Worked example · which documents are similar?

Count how often three words appear: ("math", "data", "cat").

  1. Doc A $= (2, 1, 0)$, Doc B $= (4, 2, 0)$, Doc C $= (0, 1, 3)$
    Each document becomes a vector of word counts.
  2. $\cos(A, B) = \dfrac{8 + 2 + 0}{\sqrt{5}\cdot\sqrt{20}} = \dfrac{10}{10} = 1$
    B is A written twice as long. Same topic, different length; cosine ignores length.
  3. $\cos(A, C) = \dfrac{0 + 1 + 0}{\sqrt{5}\cdot\sqrt{10}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{50}} \approx 0.14$
    Only the word "data" overlaps. Nearly unrelated (about 82°).
A and B are about the same thing; C is about something else (cats).
In real life

This is how search engines rank pages, how music apps find "more like this", and how AI chatbots look up relevant documents: everything becomes a vector, and the closest by cosine wins. A dating or roommate app that matches people by answers to the same questions can do it the same way.

Cosine similarity asks "same direction?", not "same size?".
Try it yourself (click to reveal the answer)
Find $\cos\theta$ between $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$.
Dot product $= 1$. Lengths: $1$ and $\sqrt{2}$. $\cos\theta = 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707$, so $\theta = 45°$, which matches the picture of a diagonal.

Orthogonality: completely unrelated directions

≈ 10 min

Two vectors are orthogonal (perpendicular) when their dot product is 0, because then $\cos\theta = 0$ and $\theta = 90°$.

Worked example · is $(1, 2)$ perpendicular to $(2, -1)$?
  1. $1\cdot2 + 2\cdot(-1) = 2 - 2 = 0$
    Dot product.
Yes. Zero dot product means a 90° angle.
In real life

Walking north doesn't move you east at all: north and east are orthogonal. In data, orthogonal features carry completely separate information, which is ideal: each tells you something the others can't. Noise-cancelling headphones and Wi-Fi both rely on signals that are orthogonal so they don't interfere.

Dot product zero = no overlap at all.

Key terms

Norm
A measure of a vector's length; ℓ2 is straight-line length.
Dot product
Multiply matching entries and add: $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y} = \sum_i x_i y_i$.
Cosine similarity
$\frac{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert}$: 1 for same direction, 0 for perpendicular.
Orthogonal
Perpendicular; dot product equals 0.
Lasso
A model penalized with the ℓ1 norm, which zeroes out unimportant features.
