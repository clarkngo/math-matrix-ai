Probability Spaces, Rules & Bayes' Theorem

How to reason about chance, and how to update what you believe when new evidence arrives. Counting people in a crowd makes it intuitive.

Lesson overview

Machine learning models rarely say "this email is spam". They say "this email is 87% likely to be spam". This module covers the rules for working with those numbers, and Bayes' theorem: the rule for updating a belief when you see evidence. It's the most useful, and most often misunderstood, formula in data science.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Use the complement, sum, and product rules.
  • Compute a conditional probability from a table and see why $p(A \mid B) \ne p(B \mid A)$.
  • Apply Bayes' theorem by counting a crowd of 1,000 people.
  • Compute an expected value and a variance.
  • Explain how a basic spam filter decides.

The basic rules

≈ 20 min

A probability is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). When every outcome is equally likely, it's just counting: $p(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{favorable outcomes}}{\text{all outcomes}}$.

Worked example · three rules with dice and coins
  1. $p(\text{even on a die}) = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5$
    Favorable outcomes: 2, 4, 6. Total: 6.
  2. $p(\text{not a 6}) = 1 - \frac{1}{6} = \frac{5}{6}$
    Complement rule: $p(\text{not } A) = 1 - p(A)$. Often easier than counting directly.
  3. $p(\text{1 or 2}) = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$
    Sum rule: for outcomes that can't happen together, add.
  4. $p(\text{two heads in two flips}) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$
    Product rule: for independent events (one doesn't affect the other), multiply.
In real life
  • A 4-digit PIN has $10 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10 = 10{,}000$ possibilities, so a random guess succeeds with probability $\frac{1}{10{,}000}$. Each extra digit multiplies the difficulty by 10, which is why longer passwords are safer.
  • A 30% chance of rain means a 70% chance of no rain (complement rule).
"Or" (when outcomes can't overlap) → add. "And" (when independent) → multiply. "Not" → subtract from 1.

Conditional probability: "given that…"

≈ 20 min

$p(A \mid B)$ means "the probability of $A$, given that we already know $B$ happened". Knowing $B$ shrinks the crowd you're counting to only the people where $B$ is true.

Worked example · 100 students
CodesDoesn't codeTotal
Likes math301040
Doesn't like math204060
Total5050100
  1. $p(\text{codes} \mid \text{likes math}) = \frac{30}{40} = 0.75$
    Look only at the 40 math-likers. 30 of them code.
  2. $p(\text{likes math} \mid \text{codes}) = \frac{30}{50} = 0.60$
    Now look only at the 50 coders. 30 of them like math.
Same 30 students, different crowd: $0.75 \ne 0.60$. Flipping "given" changes the answer.
In real life

"Most professional basketball players are tall" is not the same as "most tall people are professional basketball players". $p(\text{tall} \mid \text{NBA})$ is close to 1; $p(\text{NBA} \mid \text{tall})$ is tiny. Mixing these up is behind many misleading headlines.

Always ask: "Out of whom?" The thing after the bar is the crowd you count.

Bayes' theorem: updating a belief with evidence

≈ 30 min

Bayes' theorem turns $p(\text{evidence} \mid \text{cause})$, which is usually easy to measure, into $p(\text{cause} \mid \text{evidence})$, which is what you actually want:

$$p(A \mid B) = \frac{p(B \mid A)\,p(A)}{p(B)}.$$

The formula is easy to misuse. Counting a crowd is much harder to get wrong, so start there.

Worked example · a medical test, by counting 1,000 people

A disease affects 1% of people. The test catches 90% of sick people, but also flags 9% of healthy people by mistake. You test positive. How likely is it that you're sick?

  1. Sick: $1\% \times 1000 = 10$. Healthy: $990$.
    Start with the base rate, how common the disease is. This is $p(A)$, the prior.
  2. Sick and positive: $90\% \times 10 = 9$
    The test catches most sick people.
  3. Healthy but positive: $9\% \times 990 = 89.1 \approx 89$
    False alarms. A small rate applied to a big crowd gives a big number.
  4. Total positives: $9 + 89.1 = 98.1$
    Everyone who sees a positive result. This is $p(B)$.
  5. $p(\text{sick} \mid \text{positive}) = \dfrac{9}{98.1} \approx 0.092$
    Out of all positives, how many are really sick? This is the posterior.
Only about 9%, not 90%. Most positives are false alarms because the disease is rare.
In real life

This is why doctors order a second test after a positive screening, and why airport security alarms are mostly false. Fraud detection, smoke detectors, and facial recognition in a big crowd all face the same base-rate problem: when the thing you're looking for is rare, even a good detector produces mostly false alarms.

Rare things stay fairly unlikely even after a positive test. Always start from the base rate.
Try it yourself (click to reveal the answer)
Same test, but now the disease affects 10% of people. What's $p(\text{sick} \mid \text{positive})$?
Of 1,000 people: 100 sick, 900 healthy. True positives $0.9 \times 100 = 90$. False positives $0.09 \times 900 = 81$. Answer: $\frac{90}{90 + 81} = \frac{90}{171} \approx 0.53$. A more common disease makes a positive result much more meaningful.

Expected value and variance: average and spread

≈ 20 min

The expected value $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is the long-run average: multiply each outcome by its probability and add. The variance measures how spread out the outcomes are around that average.

Worked example · one roll of a die
  1. $\mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{1}{6}(1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) = \frac{21}{6} = 3.5$
    Each face has probability $\frac{1}{6}$. You can never roll 3.5, but it's the average over many rolls.
  2. $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \frac{1}{6}(1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36) = \frac{91}{6} \approx 15.17$
    Average of the squared outcomes.
  3. $\text{Var}[X] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 = 15.17 - 12.25 \approx 2.92$
    Variance = mean of squares minus square of the mean.
  4. Standard deviation $= \sqrt{2.92} \approx 1.71$
    Back in the original units: rolls typically land about 1.7 away from 3.5.
In real life

A \$2 lottery ticket with a 1-in-a-million chance of winning \$1,000,000 has expected winnings of $\frac{1}{1{,}000{,}000} \times 1{,}000{,}000 = \$1$, so on average you lose \$1 per ticket. Insurance companies price policies the same way. Variance is risk: two investments can have the same average return, but the one with higher variance swings more wildly.

Expected value = what you'd average in the long run. Variance = how bumpy the ride is.

Machine learning: a one-word spam filter

≈ 15 min

From past email: 40% of messages are spam. The word "free" appears in 50% of spam but only 5% of real email. A new email contains "free". Spam or not?

Worked example · Bayes for spam
  1. $p(\text{spam}) \cdot p(\text{"free"} \mid \text{spam}) = 0.4 \times 0.5 = 0.20$
    Share of all email that is spam and says "free".
  2. $p(\text{real}) \cdot p(\text{"free"} \mid \text{real}) = 0.6 \times 0.05 = 0.03$
    Share that is real and says "free".
  3. $p(\text{spam} \mid \text{"free"}) = \dfrac{0.20}{0.20 + 0.03} \approx 0.87$
    Among emails with "free", the spam share.
87% likely spam. A real Naive Bayes filter does this with hundreds of words at once, multiplying their evidence together.
Why this matters for ML

Classifiers output probabilities, and Bayes' theorem is how you combine prior knowledge with new evidence. Module 10 extends this: fitting a model is finding parameters that make the observed data most probable.

Key terms

Conditional probability
$p(A \mid B)$: the chance of $A$ among cases where $B$ is true.
Independent
Knowing one event tells you nothing about the other: $p(A \text{ and } B) = p(A)p(B)$.
Prior / posterior
Belief before evidence / belief after evidence.
Base rate
How common something is in the population before any test.
Expected value
Probability-weighted average of outcomes.
Variance
Average squared distance from the expected value; its square root is the standard deviation.
